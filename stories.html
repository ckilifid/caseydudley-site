Bringing in more money

Using What Was Already ThereMoney coming in wasn't keeping up with expenses, and they didn't want to borrow.

I worked with an organization with a mission behind it. They were keeping costs down, but the money coming in wasn't keeping pace with expenses. There was no excess to cut, and they didn't want to take on debt or give up part of the ownership. The only way forward was more sales, and in their line of work that doesn't happen overnight.

Looking around, I noticed they had things they owned that weren't being fully used. I started exploring whether those could become new things to sell. The early feedback was encouraging.

There was a trade-off: the new income wasn't directly tied to their mission. It doubled what they could count on each month, every quarter. Once they had built up enough savings, they were able to shift focus back to balancing their mission with staying financially healthy.

The growth came from using what they already had in a different way.

Taking care of customers

Where the Experience HappensA company that promised to handle everything for its clients.

Customers were unhappy at a company that presented itself as the kind of place where clients hand off a problem and trust it will be handled.

I walked through what a customer goes through, step by step. The first contact was impersonal, and that didn't match what the company promised. The back office leaned heavily on automated software, and people asking for help were given detailed forms to fill out about their situation, with a promise that someone would call back soon.

It turned out that a few moments in the process were causing most of the frustration.

We made the first contact quick and easy, then added a personal follow-up to have a real conversation about what the customer needed. Customer satisfaction scores improved.

The gap between what a business promises and what it delivers often sits in small, overlooked moments. Walking through what a customer goes through shows me where, so I can close those gaps instead of guessing.

When Our First Big Order Arrived DamagedOur first major sale to another business, and the product arrived damaged.

We landed our first major sale to another business, an $8 million account, and then the product arrived damaged. It was our quality control oversight.

Leadership was anxious, and we didn't have the reserves to easily absorb the cost. Legal conversations were starting, and it felt like it was heading toward a lawsuit.

Before it went further, the person running our finances and I worked through the numbers to see what emergency cash could look like. We took that to our funding partner and put it to them as a chance to show our commitment.

They approved a loan to bridge us, which gave us what we needed to make things right.

The customer noticed how we handled it. They moved forward with the next phase of the project, and later served as a reference when people considering us reached out.

People remember how we respond when things go wrong more than they remember the mistake.

Getting everyone pulling the same way

Rebuilding Trust Through TransparencyA startup whose board had lost patience with management.

The board was frustrated. Communication with management had deteriorated and decisions were slow.

After talking with the board members one at a time, I came to think the frustration was mostly about surprises, and that missed expectations had eroded trust over time.

I thought one place where everyone could see the same information might help. There was no reporting team, since it was a startup, so I figured the developers could build something. The CEO agreed and assigned it to product development. The developers emailed the board a form asking what they wanted to see. I should have anticipated that.

I stepped in and sat down with everyone, the board members and the developers, one at a time first and then together, going back and forth until we had something that worked. Board meetings became structured around it. The surprises stopped, and over time trust started to return.

When everyone is looking at the same information, trust has a better chance of coming back.

Agreeing on the Pace Before StartingA team where meetings had no agenda and every department used different tools.

Weekly meetings had no agendas and issues lingered unresolved, and each department used different tools and communicated only in reaction to problems. Trust had eroded, and people were worried about burnout.

I borrowed methods I'd seen work elsewhere, for keeping things organized and people responsible and for tracking what mattered, and customized them for the company. It should have worked, but I didn't properly set expectations with the people running the company about how long real change takes. They expected quick results, and when none showed up right away, the frustration grew. I was the wrong person in the right seat.

A good system won't work if expectations weren't set up front. Before I start, I want to find where we aren't on the same page.

Adding My Own Name to the ListJoining the leadership team of a company that was running out of money.

I joined the leadership team. In my first private conversation with the CFO, I learned the company was spending money faster than we could sustain. We'd been getting by on short-term funding that kept getting renewed, and we were running out of money. We needed to cut how fast we were spending by 40%, and fast.

I mapped who was accountable for what, starting from the outcomes the company needed and the work behind each one. It showed redundancies, and it also exposed critical gaps we'd been missing.

Even after finding the inefficiencies, the numbers still didn't add up. When I finished the restructuring plan, I added my own name to the list.

Real efficiency requires clarity about what drives outcomes and the discipline to let go of everything else.
